Premier League Scout Index · TPI

What holds up,
and what doesn’t

The TPI ranks Premier League’s 343 qualified players by attacking impact. It is there to decide who to look at: to shorten a long list, to spot who is on the way up. It is not there to forecast how many goals someone will score next month, and this page explains why.

01

What the index does

The TPI is a ranking. The checks below are about that: whether the order it produces matches what players actually did on the pitch, how early it settles, and how much it depends on the arbitrary choices of whoever built it.

Spearman ρ between the ranking as of each matchday and the end-of-season one.

0.976Order of the deciles
Split into ten groups, the index lines them up in the order of realized output. The slope, though, is 0.271 instead of 1: the order is right, the distances are not. It says who comes first, not how many times better.
0.864Holds from matchday 28
Late in the season the ranking is already close to the final one, rising to 0.967 by matchday 36. Before that it is not, and the chart above shows it: at matchday 3 it is worth 0.23. You do not have to wait for May — but you cannot trust it in September either.
0.998Weights perturbed
Randomly shifting the seven weights by ±20%, the ranking does not move (worst case 0.991). It must be said, though, that this test can hardly fail: seven positive numbers over mutually correlated z-scores, renormalised after the perturbation, give ρ ≈ 1 by construction. It is not evidence of robustness, it is a property of composite indices.
7/10And the parameter that actually matters
The seven weights are not the number that decides the ranking. The per-role coefficient (ATT 1.00 · CEN 0.85 · DIF 0.55) is — it puts z-scores computed within different roles back into a single column. Perturbed by ±20% the overall order holds (ρ 0.999), but of the top 10 only 7 names out of 10 stay put, and defenders at the top go from 1 to a range between 0 and 4. That coefficient is chosen by hand, and this is how much it moves.
0.726At team level
A squad’s mean TPI tracks the xG the team produces (20 teams, CI [+0.33, +0.91]). It holds against an outcome that has nothing to do with the individual player.
0.803First half → second
Players who deliver in the first half also deliver in the second (517 players, CI [+0.76, +0.84], placebo p = 0.002). It concerns raw attacking output, the index’s main ingredient: it is not proof about the composite.

From the first to the tenth decile

Mean realized output from the first to the last TPI decile. The dashed line is the steady rise a calibrated index would show: the order reverses at 2 points (1–2, 3–4), and the spacing never matches. Monotonicity ρ = +0.976.

02

The test built to fail it

An index is judged against the most trivial alternative that could replace it. I froze the ranking at matchdays 25, 28, 30, 33, 36 and asked the TPI and three elementary predictors to guess output over the matchdays that followed, which at that point had not been played yet.

This section is the only one that can fail the index, and in part it does. I report it in full because an index that has never lost is just an index that has never been tested.

-0.0348Against raw output
The TPI does not beat it: the interval [-0.045, -0.025] sits entirely on the wrong side. To forecast the next half-season’s goals, that statistic alone does better than the index built around it.
pariAgainst minutes and value
Against minutes played and market value the comparison ends level: the intervals contain zero. An index built on seven dimensions does no better than how much a player plays, or what the market pays for him.
+0.0064On who improves
Change the question — not how much he will produce, but who will improve on himself — and the TPI beats raw output (goals + xa)/90 and minutes played and market value, with the interval entirely above zero. It is the only ground where it wins, and it is the one the index was built for.
-0.0016The scout modulators
Age and physical reliability, added on top of the TPI, move nothing out-of-sample: [-0.0036, +0.0002] across 1538 comparisons. They help read a player’s profile, not forecast his output.
2 su 7Dimensions that do not pay
Removed one at a time, most dimensions improve the forecast: they weigh on the ranking but not on the outcome. The ones that earn their place are output_adj (-0.069) and finishing (-0.054) and buildup_adj (-0.009) and boost_ratio (-0.007) and centralita (-0.005).
+0.050Averaging two seasons
A TPI averaged over two seasons ranks better than a single-season one (+0.15 → +0.20 over 73 players), but the interval [-0.059, +0.159] grazes zero: I do not count it.

In two lines: as a forecast of absolute output the index is of no use, and raw per-90 output does better on its own. As a way to tell who is rising from who is fading it holds, and that is the only use I claim — the one the index was written for.

03

What proves nothing

Four comparisons I keep because they say something about the index, not because they prove it. None of these numbers can fail it: either I decide who enters the comparison, or the criterion is built so that any result sounds good. They would be the easiest ones to show off, which is exactly why they sit at the bottom.

0.283317 players, market value
The index and the market agree in part, and that is the good news: market value also carries age, contract length and reputation, not just this season’s output. A high agreement would mean this index is a complicated way of sorting players by price. Source: heXI / Transfermarkt.
-0.064Age against quality
The age index is uncorrelated with the TPI, and it should be: it measures the point of a career, not how good a player is. If the two moved together, one of them would be redundant.
0.924TPI against TPI Pro
High by construction: the Pro version contains the TPI. I put it here because someone would quote it as evidence that the Pro works, and it is not. That answer is in the previous section.
+0.889The best role
By role the link with output is highest for CEN (80 players). Read it with care: the criterion is attacking output, so within defence it separates attacking full-backs, not good defenders.
04

What follows

A page that only lists problems is half a page. This is what the results above leave me to do, and what I have decided not to do.

The weights
Five dimensions out of seven do not pay against future output (consistenza, form). The obvious move is to cut their weight in favour of output_adj and finishing and buildup_adj and boost_ratio and centralita. I have not done it, and that is a choice: the criterion that fails them is the forecast of absolute output, which is not what the index is for. Retuning on that criterion would mean chasing something the index does not promise. It becomes the first thing to redo the day the TPI has to feed something that consumes distances and not just order.
The distances
The 0.271 slope says TPI differences are inflated by roughly 3.7×. As long as the index is read as a ranking that changes nothing; if it ever feeds a valuation model, it has to be rescaled first.
What is missing to answer
The hypothesis the index was born from — spotting who is entering their prime rather than who is already there — is neither proved nor disproved: judging it would mean following the same players for years, and there are two seasons here (73 players appear in both). The next useful step is not another test: it is backfilling more seasons.
05

How it is measured

Sample

The checks run on all 343 qualified players, not on the dashboard’s top hundred. That cut is a product choice: comparing only the best against each other narrows the variance and lowers every correlation by itself, so it would measure the selection rather than the index.

Out-of-sample

The predictive tests start from snapshots of the ranking at matchdays 25, 28, 30, 33, 36: the predictors come from there, the criterion from the matches that followed. None of the criterion’s data is available to whoever makes the prediction.

Uncertainty

Every figure on this page carries a 95% bootstrap confidence interval. Where the same player appears in several comparisons, the resampling keeps his rows together: treating them as independent would shrink the intervals and make non-significant results look significant.

Thresholds

What counts as a result is decided before looking at the numbers, and lives in a single place in the code. Where a cut-off would make no sense — the baseline comparison, persistence — the sign of the confidence interval decides: above zero it holds, below it does not, straddling zero is a tie. The only numeric cut concerns the backtest, where I read |r| ≥ 0.50 as strong and ≥ 0.30 as moderate. When a result crosses the rule, what changes is what I write, not the rule: that happened to cross-season persistence, which used to count as a gain and no longer does.

Limits

Goalkeepers are not in the index: the TPI measures attacking impact — xG, xA, finishing, involvement in build-up — and for a goalkeeper those quantities are undefined. Rating them needs a different index, not a different weight in this one. The market value used as a baseline is an end-of-season snapshot, so it already «knows» how the season went: it enters the comparison with an advantage. The first-half/second-half backtest measures raw attacking output, not the composite.

Data

A MySQL database with 23057 player-match rows over 756 players, two seasons of Premier League. xG and xA come from Understat, biographical data and market value from Transfermarkt, joined on identifiers rather than names. Fantacalcio and WhoScored ratings are the only hand-entered data. The engine is Python: pandas for the transformations, scipy for the statistics, and a regression suite that runs on every change.

Code

The engine that computes the index is not public: what I publish are the pages and the data they run on. The payload.json file in the site repository holds, player by player, every dimension and score this page uses: anyone who wants to redo the arithmetic can do it from there without taking my word for it. I am happy to walk through the code on request.
